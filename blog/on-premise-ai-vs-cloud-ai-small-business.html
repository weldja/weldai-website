On-Premise AI vs Cloud AI: A Plain-English Guide for Small Businesses

By James Weld November 2026 5 min read
JW
James Weld
Weld AI · I install private, on-premise AI for UK small businesses

"On-premise" is an old IT term for a simple idea: the software runs on a computer in your own building, not on someone else's computer over the internet. Applied to AI, it means the AI model itself runs in your office.

For years that was only realistic for large companies with server rooms. It is now possible on a good office PC, which makes it a real choice for a small business. This guide explains the difference and helps you decide which you need.

What happens when you use cloud AI

When you type a question into ChatGPT, Copilot or Gemini, your words travel over the internet to a data centre, usually run by a large American technology company. The AI model runs there, and the answer travels back. If you attach a document, the document goes too.

That is not sinister. It is simply how the service works, and for most everyday tasks it is fine. The providers publish terms about how long they keep your data and whether they use it to improve their models, and those terms differ between free, personal and business plans.

The point is that you are trusting a third party with whatever you send. For a letter to a supplier, that hardly matters. For a client's tax return, a disciplinary file or a contract under negotiation, it matters a great deal.

What happens when you use on-premise AI

With on-premise AI, the model is installed on a computer you own. Your question goes from your keyboard to that computer and no further. There is no data centre, no provider and no internet connection involved.

The simplest test of whether AI is truly on-premise: unplug the internet. If it still answers, it is.

The honest trade-offs

On-premise AI is not better at everything, and you should be wary of anyone who says it is.

  • It is slower. A data centre has far more powerful hardware than any office. An office PC with a suitable graphics card starts answering in about ten seconds. Without a graphics card, it can take up to a minute.
  • The model is smaller. The models that fit on a PC are less capable than the largest cloud models. They are well suited to one job: reading passages from your documents and answering from them accurately. They are not the tool for writing a novel.
  • You need the right PC. Memory and the graphics card matter. A free system check will tell you whether a PC you already own is suitable.

What you get in return is certainty. You do not have to read a provider's terms, check which plan your staff are on, or explain to a client where their data went. It went nowhere.

Which should a small business choose?

Most businesses will sensibly use both, for different things.

Cloud AI suits

  • drafting emails, marketing copy and general writing
  • research on public information
  • anything you would be comfortable posting on a noticeboard

On-premise AI suits

  • questions about your own client files, contracts and accounts
  • HR records and anything containing personal data
  • work covered by professional confidentiality rules
  • businesses whose clients or insurers ask where their data is processed
  • sites with a poor or restricted internet connection

A useful rule for staff: if you would not email it to a stranger, do not paste it into a cloud AI tool. Give them an on-premise alternative for those documents and the rule becomes easy to follow.
